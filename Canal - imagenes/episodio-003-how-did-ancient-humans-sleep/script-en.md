# 003 — How Did Ancient Humans Sleep?

- **Formato**: explainer ~10 min, monigotes MS Paint, segunda persona.
- **Fenómeno con nombre**: segmented sleep / biphasic sleep (sueño segmentado).
- **Portada (idea)**: monigote grande de frente, despierto de noche con cara de fastidio, hoguera pequeña a sus pies, fondo azul noche con franja de suelo. Texto: "TWICE A NIGHT?"
- **Narración**: solo se locuta lo que va bajo `NARRATION:`.

---

## COLD OPEN

NARRATION:
It's three in the morning and you're awake.
Not sick. Not worried about anything in particular. Just awake, staring at the ceiling, doing the arithmetic — if I fall asleep right now I still get four hours — which is the exact thought that guarantees you won't.
And somewhere in there is the quiet fear that something is wrong with you. That normal people sleep through the night, and you have somehow lost the ability.
Here's the thing.
For most of human history, waking up in the middle of the night wasn't the problem.
It was the schedule.

## ACT 1 — THE FIRST SLEEP AND THE SECOND SLEEP

NARRATION:
In the nineteen nineties, a historian called Roger Ekirch was digging through old documents — court records, diaries, medical books, letters, plays — looking for something completely unrelated.
And he kept tripping over the same odd phrase.
First sleep.
Somebody would write that a thing happened "after the first sleep." A witness would testify that she got up "between the first and the second sleep." A doctor would advise taking a particular remedy "at first waking."
He started collecting them. He found hundreds. More than five hundred references, across centuries, in English, French, Italian, Latin. Different countries, different classes, different eras, all casually referring to two sleeps as if everybody obviously knew what that meant.
Because everybody did.
Before artificial light was cheap, an ordinary night in much of Europe went like this. You went to bed not long after dark. You slept for three or four hours. Then you woke up — not by accident, not because something disturbed you, but simply because that was the shape of the night. You were awake for an hour, maybe two. Then you went back to bed and slept until morning.
Nobody wrote about this as a problem. Nobody wrote about it much at all, in the way that nobody writes down that they breathe.
The most striking thing about all five hundred references is that not one of them bothers to explain it.

## ACT 2 — THE HOUR IN THE MIDDLE

NARRATION:
So what did people actually do in the middle of the night?
Everything, apparently.
They talked. Couples lying in the dark, in the quietest hour of the day, with no children awake and no work to do. Physicians of the period specifically recommended that hour for sex, on the grounds that people were rested and would do a better job of it.
They prayed. There are prayer manuals written specifically for that gap.
They got up and tended the fire, checked on animals, mended things by the light of the embers. Some people went and visited the neighbours. There are court records of people committing perfectly ordinary crimes at two in the morning and no one finding the hour suspicious.
And a lot of them just lay there in a strange half-awake state, thinking. Ekirch found that people used that hour to interpret their dreams, because they had just come out of the deepest stretch of dreaming and it was all still there.
That last detail is worth sitting with. An entire culture of dream interpretation may exist partly because people used to wake up in the middle of the night with the dream still warm.
We don't remember our dreams as well as our ancestors did.
Not because our dreams are worse. Because we sleep straight through them.

## ACT 3 — THE EXPERIMENT THAT BROUGHT IT BACK

NARRATION:
Now, that's history. Documents can be wrong, and people can describe things they don't actually do.
So in the early nineteen nineties, a sleep researcher named Thomas Wehr tried something direct. He took a group of volunteers and gave them a long artificial winter night. Fourteen hours of darkness, every night, for a month. No lamps, no screens, nothing to do but lie there.
For the first few weeks the volunteers mostly slept off an accumulated debt — long, heavy nights.
And then something changed.
Their sleep split in two.
They would fall asleep, sleep for about four hours, wake up for one to three hours, and then sleep again until the light came. Nobody instructed them to. It just settled into that shape once the night was long enough.
And the hour in between was not like being awake at three in the morning in your bedroom now. The volunteers described it as calm. Quiet. Almost meditative. Their bodies were releasing elevated levels of prolactin — the same hormone associated with the still, contented state of an animal resting.
It wasn't insomnia. It was a third thing. A state most people alive today have simply never been in.

## ACT 4 — SOMEBODY HAS TO BE AWAKE

NARRATION:
There's a second theory about broken sleep, and it comes from a completely different direction. Not from history. From the problem of not being eaten.
If you're a group of humans sleeping on the ground, in the open, surrounded by things with teeth, then a night where everyone is unconscious at the same time is a spectacularly bad idea. Somebody should be awake.
Researchers tested this with the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer community in Tanzania. They tracked the sleep of a group of adults across many nights and a couple of hundred hours of data, and then asked a simple question: how much of that time was every single person asleep at once?
The answer was eighteen minutes.
Out of more than two hundred hours, there were eighteen minutes where the whole camp was out.
And they weren't taking shifts. Nobody was assigned a watch. It happened by itself, because people of different ages naturally drift to different schedules. Older adults went to bed early and woke early. Younger adults stayed up late and slept in.
Which suggests something a bit lovely about a complaint you've definitely heard. Older people who wake at four in the morning and can't get back to sleep aren't broken. Teenagers who can't fall asleep before two aren't lazy.
Between them, they cover the whole night.
That's not a malfunction. That may be the point.

## ACT 5 — BUT HERE'S THE COMPLICATION

NARRATION:
Now I have to be honest with you, because this is where a lot of videos about this stop, and they shouldn't.
Those same hunter-gatherer studies found something inconvenient.
The Hadza in Tanzania, the San in Namibia, the Tsimané in Bolivia — three groups on two continents, living without electric light — do not sleep in two blocks. They sleep once, for somewhere between six and seven hours, straight through. Less than the eight we're told to get.
And none of the three groups has a word for insomnia.
So the two-sleep pattern that Ekirch found in all those documents may not be the ancient human default at all. It may be something specific to long, cold, dark European winters, where the night lasts fourteen hours and no human being can sleep for all of it.
Which leaves us with two true things that don't quite fit together. Pre-industrial Europeans clearly slept twice. Equatorial hunter-gatherers clearly don't.
The honest read is that there is no single natural way to sleep. Sleep bends to the length of the night, the temperature, the danger, and who else is in the room.
What there definitely isn't, anywhere in the historical or anthropological record, is the thing you've been told is normal.

## ACT 6 — WHO INVENTED THE EIGHT-HOUR BLOCK

NARRATION:
So where did the eight-hour block come from?
Light, mostly. Cheap, bright, artificial light.
Street lighting spread through European cities and the night stopped being a wall you hit at sunset. Then gas, then electricity, and bedtime slid later and later. The evening — the whole idea of an evening, as a thing you do rather than a thing you sleep through — is a fairly recent invention.
And the night got squeezed from both ends. Factory work arrived, with clocks and shifts and a fixed hour to be somewhere. Sleep stopped being a natural shape and became a slot.
By the early twentieth century, references to the first and second sleep had almost completely vanished from the language. Within a couple of generations, people forgot it had ever been a thing.
And then something worse happened. The consolidated block didn't just become normal. It became the standard. The thing you're supposed to achieve. Eight hours, uninterrupted, or you're doing it wrong.
So now, when your body wakes you at three in the morning, you don't experience it as a quiet hour.
You experience it as failure.

## ACT 7 — WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS FOR YOU

NARRATION:
Which brings us back to you, lying there, doing the arithmetic.
There is a real thing called sleep-maintenance insomnia and it is genuinely miserable and worth treating, so this isn't a suggestion that everybody's three a.m. is fine. If you're exhausted every day, go and see someone about it.
But there's a piece of this that's worth knowing anyway.
A lot of what makes night-waking harmful isn't the waking. It's the panic about the waking. The clock-watching, the arithmetic, the certainty that tomorrow is ruined. That's the part that dumps stress hormones into a body that was trying to be calm, and that's the part that keeps you up for two hours instead of twenty minutes.
Your ancestors woke at the same hour you do. They just didn't lie there thinking that something had gone wrong.
They thought it was the middle of the night.
Because it was.

## RESOLUTION

NARRATION:
So the next time you find yourself awake at three in the morning, consider the possibility that nothing is broken.
You've just arrived in the gap. The hour that used to be for talking, and praying, and remembering your dreams, and keeping half an eye on the dark while everyone else slept.
Nobody uses it for that anymore. There's a phone next to you and it's very bright and it will happily take the hour off your hands.
But it's still there. Every night. Waiting.
And if you're now wondering what else about your body you've been told is a problem when it isn't...
...that's a question for another video.
