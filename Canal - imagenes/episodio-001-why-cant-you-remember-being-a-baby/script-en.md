# 001 — Why Can't You Remember Being a Baby?

- **Formato**: explainer animado ~8 min, monigotes, segunda persona.
- **Fenómeno con nombre**: Infantile Amnesia (amnesia infantil).
- **Miniatura (idea)**: monigote adulto mirando una cuna vacía, texto amarillo bold: "WHO WERE YOU?"
- **Narración**: las líneas `NARRATION` son el texto que se envía a ElevenLabs tal cual. Las líneas `VISUAL` son dirección de animación, no se locutan.
- **Duración estimada**: ~1.200 palabras ≈ 8 min a ritmo conversacional.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00 – 0:25)

VISUAL: Black screen. A single stick figure baby appears, giggling in a crib. Hard cut to the same figure as an adult, staring at the camera, confused.

NARRATION:
You were alive for about three years... that you have zero memory of.
You laughed. You cried. You learned to walk, to talk, to recognize your own name. Someone fed you every single day. You had a favorite toy. You had entire relationships.
And it's all gone.
Not blurry. Not faded. Gone.
So here's the question: if you can't remember any of it... who was that baby?

## ACT 1 — THE MYSTERY (0:25 – 1:45)

VISUAL: Adult stick figure walking backwards along a timeline labeled with ages. At "age 3-4" the timeline just... stops. Beyond it, white fog.

NARRATION:
Try it right now. What's your earliest memory?
Go ahead, I'll wait.
Whatever you just thought of — a birthday, a beach, falling off something — odds are you were about three and a half years old. That's the average. Almost nobody remembers anything from before age two. And between two and four? Just fragments. Random flashes with no story attached.
Scientists call this infantile amnesia, and here's why it's genuinely weird: those first years are the most important years of your entire life. You learned more, faster, than you ever will again. A language from scratch. How to control your own body. Who your family is.
Your brain recorded the most valuable data it will ever collect...
...and then apparently threw it in the trash.
Why?

## ACT 2 — THE OBVIOUS ANSWER IS WRONG (1:45 – 3:15)

VISUAL: A stick figure baby in a lab, lying under a crib mobile. A ribbon connects the baby's ankle to the mobile. Baby kicks — mobile spins — baby's eyes go wide with delight.

NARRATION:
Your first guess is probably: "babies just can't form memories yet. Their brains aren't finished."
Nice theory. It's wrong.
In the 1980s, psychologist Carolyn Rovee-Collier ran an experiment that's equal parts adorable and mind-blowing. She tied a ribbon from a baby's ankle to a mobile hanging over the crib. The baby kicks, the mobile moves. Babies love this. They figure it out fast.
Here's the twist: bring the same baby back weeks later, same crib, same mobile — no ribbon. The baby immediately starts kicking.
It remembered.
Two-month-olds held onto that memory for days. Six-month-olds, for weeks. Babies form memories just fine. They can remember people, places, routines, even sequences of events.
So the recordings existed. Your brain made them.
Which makes the real question so much creepier: where did they go?

## ACT 3 — THE DELETION (3:15 – 5:00)

VISUAL: Inside a stick figure's head: a filing room labeled "HIPPOCAMPUS". Tiny worker figures are adding brand-new shelves — and as they install them, old files fall off the back and dissolve.

NARRATION:
Meet your hippocampus. It's the part of your brain that files away memories of events — what scientists call episodic memory. Your first kiss, your worst haircut, that thing you said in 2014 that still haunts you at 3 a.m. All filed here.
And in a baby, the hippocampus has a problem: it's still under construction.
It's one of the only parts of the brain that keeps growing brand-new neurons at full speed — a process called neurogenesis. Sounds great, right? New neurons, new learning power.
Except in 2014, researchers found the catch. When new neurons wire themselves into the hippocampus, they don't politely slot in at the end. They barge into existing circuits — the circuits already holding your memories.
When scientists boosted neurogenesis in adult mice, the mice started forgetting things they'd already learned. When they slowed neurogenesis in baby mice, the babies remembered longer.
Read that again.
The very process building your brain was shredding the files as fast as you saved them. You didn't fail to record your babyhood.
Your own brain recorded over it. Like a security tape on a loop.

## ACT 4 — THE FREUD DETOUR (5:00 – 5:50)

VISUAL: A stick figure with a cigar and a notepad, nodding way too confidently. A big red "NOPE" stamp slams over him.

NARRATION:
Quick historical detour, because the first guy to name this problem had a very different theory.
Sigmund Freud noticed infantile amnesia over a century ago. His explanation? You do remember your early childhood — but it was so full of disturbing desires that your mind repressed all of it. Buried it in your subconscious. On purpose.
Very dramatic, Sigmund.
There's basically no evidence for that. But to be fair to the man with the cigar: he spotted something real that everyone else had ignored. The memories aren't missing because nothing happened.
Something is actively keeping them from you.
He just got the "something" wrong.

## ACT 5 — THE TWIST: YOUR FIRST MEMORY IS PROBABLY FAKE (5:50 – 7:00)

VISUAL: The adult stick figure proudly holding a framed "MY FIRST MEMORY" picture. The picture slowly glitches — revealing it's actually a photo from a family album, with mom narrating off-screen.

NARRATION:
Now for the part of the video that might genuinely upset you.
That earliest memory you came up with a few minutes ago?
There's a decent chance it never happened.
In a study of over six thousand people, researchers found that nearly forty percent reported a "first memory" from before age two — an age where, as we've seen, lasting episodic memories basically can't survive. Those memories weren't recordings. They were reconstructions. Built later — from family stories, photo albums, and imagination — and then quietly filed under "things I actually experienced."
Your brain doesn't play back memories like video. It rebuilds them from scratch every single time you remember. And it will happily use whatever materials it finds lying around.
Here's the cruelest detail: small children do remember being babies. Ask a three-year-old about something from age one and a half, and sometimes they'll tell you. But around age seven, those early memories start dissolving — fast.
Which means at some point in your childhood, you held the last surviving memory of your own babyhood in your head...
...remembered it one final time...
...and never thought about it again.

## ACT 6 — RESOLUTION (7:00 – 8:00)

VISUAL: Split screen. Left: the baby stacking blocks, falling, hugging a parent. Right: the adult — and each baby moment lights up a glowing layer inside the adult's silhouette: language, confidence, trust.

NARRATION:
So those three missing years — are they just... gone?
Not exactly. Because your brain has more than one memory system, and only one of them got wiped.
The events were deleted. But the skills stayed. The language you're listening to right now? Installed during the missing years. The way you walk. Your sense of whether the world is basically safe or basically threatening. Whether being held feels like comfort. All of it was written during the years you can't access — not as memories, but as the operating system you run on.
You can't remember being that baby.
But you never actually lost them.
You're just running everything they built.

VISUAL: The adult figure looks back at the empty crib one last time — smiles — lights out.

NARRATION:
And if you're now wondering whether the rest of your memories are trustworthy...
...that's a question for another video.
