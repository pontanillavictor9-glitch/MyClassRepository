# 002 — Why Do You Look Worse in Photos?

- **Formato**: explainer ~10 min, monigotes MS Paint, segunda persona.
- **Fenómeno con nombre**: mere-exposure effect (efecto de mera exposición).
- **Miniatura (idea)**: monigote mirando un espejo y una foto, con caras distintas. Texto amarillo: "NOT YOUR FACE".
- **Narración**: solo se locuta lo que va bajo `NARRATION:`.

---

## COLD OPEN

NARRATION:
There are two versions of your face.
One of them you have seen every single day of your life. You have studied it, adjusted it, decided how you feel about it.
The other one is the one everybody else sees.
And the first time you really notice the gap — a photo somebody took of you without asking, at an angle you did not choose — it feels like a small betrayal. That can't be me. I don't look like that.
Here's the uncomfortable part.
You don't look like the mirror either.
So which one is lying?

## ACT 1 — THE MIRROR IS BACKWARDS

NARRATION:
Start with the obvious thing that almost nobody actually thinks about.
A mirror flips you.
Not upside down. Left to right. The mole on your left cheek sits on the right side of the reflection. The eyebrow that arches a little higher, the tooth that leans slightly in, the way your smile pulls harder on one side — all of it is on the wrong side.
And you have been staring at that reversed face since you were about eighteen months old. Thousands of hours of it. Every mirror, every shop window, every phone held up in selfie mode — because your phone flips the preview too, to feel natural to you.
That reversed face is the one your brain filed away as you. It is the reference image. It is what you check yourself against.
Now a camera takes a picture. Somebody else's camera, or your own phone quietly un-flipping the image when it saves it.
And what comes out is the face everybody else has been looking at your entire life.
It is not a worse face. It is a stranger's face. And your brain, which is extraordinarily good at detecting when a face is subtly wrong, immediately puts its hand up.
Something about this is off.
Something is off. It's you. You are the thing that's off.

## ACT 2 — THE STUDY THAT SETTLES IT

NARRATION:
In nineteen seventy-seven, three researchers ran an experiment that settles this cleanly.
They photographed a group of people. Then they made two prints of every face: the normal photograph, and a mirror-reversed version of that exact same photograph. Same person, same light, same expression. The only difference was which way round it was.
Then they showed both to the person in the picture and asked which one they preferred.
People overwhelmingly chose the mirror version. Their own reversed face. The one that does not exist anywhere in the world except in reflections.
Then the researchers showed the same two prints to that person's close friends and partners.
The friends chose the other one. The real one.
Everybody preferred the version they were used to.
This is one of the most reliable findings in psychology, and it has a name: the mere-exposure effect. It is almost embarrassingly simple. The more times you see something, the more you like it. Not because it is good. Just because it is familiar. It works on faces, on songs, on logos, even on nonsense words in a language you do not speak.
So when a photo of you comes out and you feel that small drop in your stomach — that is not your aesthetic judgement talking. That is a familiarity alarm going off.
Your brain is not saying "this is ugly."
It is saying "this is not the one."

## ACT 3 — YOUR FACE IS NOT SYMMETRICAL

NARRATION:
But hold on. If a mirror only flips you left to right, and your face were symmetrical, then flipping would change absolutely nothing. You would look identical either way.
Which means this entire problem rests on one fact: nobody's face is symmetrical. Not yours. Not anybody's.
One eye sits slightly higher. One side of the jaw is a fraction wider. One nostril is bigger. Your smile pulls harder to one side — almost everyone's does. Even your hair has a side it wants to fall on.
Normally you never notice any of it, because you only ever see one version. But flip the image, and every single one of those small asymmetries jumps to the other side of your face at the same moment. Individually, each one is nothing. Together they add up to a face that is recognisably you, and somehow not quite you.
There is a demonstration of this you can do yourself in about a minute. Take a straight-on photo of your face. Cut it down the middle. Mirror each half to build two complete faces out of it.
You get two people. Two people who clearly look related. But not the same person.
And you will almost certainly like one of them noticeably more than the other.
Which is a strange thing to discover about a face you have owned your entire life.

## ACT 4 — THE LENS IS LYING TOO

NARRATION:
Now, the mirror explains why photos of you feel wrong. It does not explain why some photos of you are so much worse than others.
That part is not psychology at all. That part is just optics.
A lens has a focal length, and focal length changes the shape of a face. It is not the camera being unkind to you. It is geometry.
When an object is close to a lens, the parts nearest the camera get exaggerated relative to the parts further away. And on a face, the thing nearest the camera is your nose.
Surgeons have actually measured this, because people were bringing selfies into consultations. Take a photo at arm's length — roughly thirty centimetres — and your nose comes out looking about thirty percent wider than it really is. Move the camera back to a metre and a half and photograph the same face again, and that distortion almost entirely disappears.
That is exactly why portrait photographers stand further back and zoom in, instead of walking closer.
Which means the phone in your hand at arm's length — the way the overwhelming majority of photographs of you get taken — is running the single most unflattering configuration available to it.
Your face did not change between the mirror and the photograph.
The maths did.

## ACT 5 — THE FROZEN FRAME

NARRATION:
There is a third thing happening, and this one is my favourite, because once you see it you cannot unsee it.
In the mirror, you are moving.
You are never actually looking at a still image of yourself. You are looking at a live feed, and you are adjusting it in real time. You tilt your chin down a fraction. You settle your mouth into the position you long ago decided is your face. You look at yourself in the specific way you have practised looking at yourself.
You have never once seen yourself in a mirror mid-sentence. Or halfway through a blink. Or in the first quarter-second of reacting to something.
A camera captures one two-hundredth of a second, and it does not wait for a good one. It takes whatever your face happened to be doing at that instant — including all the in-between positions your face passes through on the way from one expression to another, which nobody has ever seen frozen. Including you.
And there is research on precisely this. Film someone, then pull a single frame out of the footage, and people rate the moving version as better-looking than the still — even though every frame in that video is itself a still.
Movement flatters. Freezing does not.
So you are not comparing your face to your face.
You are comparing a curated live performance to a random screenshot of it.

## ACT 6 — THE CAMERA CHANGES YOUR FACE

NARRATION:
And then comes the final insult, which is that being photographed changes what your face is actually doing.
The moment somebody points a camera at you, you start performing a face. You put on the smile you believe you have.
And a deliberate smile uses different muscles than a real one. A genuine smile crinkles the muscles around the eyes, and most people simply cannot fire those on command. So the posed smile lands as very slightly wrong — and other people can tell, even when they could not tell you why.
Then add the conditions nobody chose. Overhead lighting that drops shadows under your eyes. A flash that flattens your whole face into a pancake. Somebody shooting from below.
And then they post it.

## ACT 7 — SO WHICH ONE IS REAL?

NARRATION:
So. Which one is you?
The photograph is closer. It is the orientation everybody else sees, and it is the version that actually exists out in the world. But it has lens distortion and a frozen frame and a performed smile stacked on top of it, so it is not clean either.
The mirror is the right shape and the wrong way round.
The honest answer is that the most accurate version of your face is the one nobody ever shows you. You, moving, at a normal distance, un-flipped.
Which is to say: the version that every single person who has ever met you has been looking at the entire time.
And here is the part that should actually land.
The people who love you are not carrying the mirror version of you around in their heads. They never saw it. They fell for the other one — the one in the photograph you hate.
That is the face they picture when they think about you. And it has never once looked wrong to them.
Because to them, it is the only face you have ever had.

## RESOLUTION

NARRATION:
So the next time a photo of you appears and your stomach drops, pay attention to what your brain is actually reporting.
It is not reporting ugliness. It is reporting unfamiliarity. And it uses the exact same alarm for both, which is precisely why the two feel identical from the inside.
You have just met the version of yourself that everybody else has known for years.
They are not disappointed by it.
And if you are now wondering how many other things about yourself you have been seeing backwards this whole time...
...that is a question for another video.
