# 006 — Do Animals Know They're Going to Die?

- **Formato**: explainer ~10 min, monigotes MS Paint, segunda persona.
- **Campo**: tanatología comparada.
- **Portada (idea)**: monigote-elefante grande de frente con cara triste tocando un hueso pequeño en el suelo. Texto: "SHE KNOWS?"
- **Narración**: solo se locuta lo que va bajo `NARRATION:`.

---

## COLD OPEN

NARRATION:
An elephant walking across the savannah comes across a pile of bones.
She stops. The whole group stops. They gather round, and they start touching — running the tips of their trunks over the skull, over the tusks, lifting pieces and turning them over. Some of them put their feet on the bones very gently. It goes on for several minutes.
Then they move on.
They do this for elephant bones. They do not do it for buffalo bones, or rhino bones, or a piece of wood.
So she knows something. The question is what.
Does she know that this was one of us? Does she know what happened to it? And does she know that one day it will happen to her?

## ACT 1 — WHAT THE QUESTION ACTUALLY IS

NARRATION:
We need to be careful here, because there are three different questions hiding inside this one and people mix them up constantly.
The first is: do animals react to death? Obviously yes. That's not in dispute and it isn't very interesting.
The second is: do animals grieve? That's harder, but the behaviour is well documented and it looks a lot like grief.
The third is the real one. Does an animal understand death as a concept? That it's permanent. That it happens to everyone. And — the big one — that it is going to happen to me, personally, at some point in a future I can imagine.
Developmental psychologists actually break this down, because they study when human children get it. There are separate components. That the body stops working. That it can't be undone. That it happens to every living thing. That there are physical causes. And that it applies to you.
Children pick these up in pieces, usually somewhere between four and seven. The last one to arrive is almost always the personal one.
So we're not asking whether animals are sad. We're asking how much of that list they have.

## ACT 2 — THE BONES

NARRATION:
Back to the elephants, because it's the best-studied case.
Researchers laid out different objects for wild elephant families to find: elephant skulls, rhino skulls, buffalo skulls, pieces of ivory, and plain wood.
The elephants went for the ivory and the elephant skulls. They spent significantly more time investigating them, touching and smelling them, than anything else on offer.
So they can tell. An elephant skull registers as a different category of object to an elephant than a buffalo skull does.
And this isn't a one-off. Elephants have been recorded standing over dead members of their group for hours, trying to lift them, covering them with earth and branches, returning to the place afterwards.
There are accounts of elephants staying with a body through the night, and of a group refusing to leave until the smell has changed. Keepers in sanctuaries describe animals going off their food for days after a death in the herd.
Now — the honest reading. All of that shows recognition and something that looks a great deal like distress. It does not, by itself, show an understanding that death is permanent. An elephant trying to lift a dead companion might be doing so precisely because it does not understand that this is over. It might be trying to wake her up.
Which is a strange and slightly awful thought, and it is going to come back later in this video.

## ACT 3 — THE MOTHERS

NARRATION:
Then there's the case that went round the world.
In twenty eighteen, an orca off the coast of Washington gave birth. The calf lived about half an hour.
And the mother did not let it sink. She carried the body on her head, pushing it up through the water, and when it slipped off she dived and retrieved it. She did this for seventeen days, across more than a thousand kilometres, while her family group stayed with her and — according to the observers — at times helped carry it.
She stopped eating properly. She fell behind the group. The other whales waited.
This behaviour is not unique to her. Primatologists have documented chimpanzee and macaque mothers carrying dead infants for days and sometimes weeks, continuing to groom them, until the body is unrecognisable.
It is very hard to watch that and conclude nothing is happening inside.
But notice what it does not tell us. Carrying a dead infant for seventeen days is, if anything, evidence of not understanding permanence. It is what a mother does when the alarm that says my baby is missing has no way to be switched off.
The grief may be real. That doesn't mean the concept is.

## ACT 4 — THE CROWS, AND WHY THEY'RE DIFFERENT

NARRATION:
Now for a case that looks like a funeral and turns out to be something much more practical.
When a crow finds a dead crow, other crows come. They gather in the trees around it, they call loudly, and a crowd builds. It has been called a crow funeral for a hundred years.
So researchers tested it properly. They had a person hold a dead crow while standing in a particular spot, wearing a distinctive mask.
The crows mobbed and alarm-called. Fine. But then came the interesting part.
For weeks afterwards, those crows avoided that place. And they remembered the face. When the same mask appeared later, without any dead crow, they scolded it.
So it isn't mourning. It's intelligence gathering. A dead crow is information: something killed a crow here, and possibly that thing is standing right there. Learn the location, learn the face, tell everybody, don't go back.
This is arguably more impressive than grief. It's an animal using another animal's death as data about the world.
But it tells you nothing about whether the crow knows it's mortal.

## ACT 5 — THE MISSING INGREDIENT

NARRATION:
So what would an animal actually need, in order to know that it is going to die?
Not sadness. Not memory. It needs to be able to place itself in a future that does not exist yet, and imagine a state of the world it will never experience.
That's a very specific cognitive trick, and it's the same one that lets you dread a dentist appointment on Thursday or feel nervous about a conversation that hasn't happened.
For a long time the assumption was that only humans do this. That animals live in a permanent present, dealing with what's in front of them.
That assumption is now in trouble.
Scrub jays hide food. And in experiments, they hide food for a tomorrow they are not currently experiencing — given a situation where they'll be hungry in a particular room in the morning, they'll cache food in that room the night before. They aren't responding to hunger now. They're solving a problem they will have later.
Great apes have been shown selecting and keeping a tool they'll need for a task hours in the future.
So the ability to think about the future isn't uniquely ours. It's a matter of degree.
And that makes the line we drew a lot blurrier than it was.

## ACT 6 — SO WHAT'S THE HONEST ANSWER?

NARRATION:
Here it is, and it is going to be less satisfying than you want.
We don't know. And there's a decent argument that we can't know, because the only way to be sure would be to ask, and the answer would have to come back in language.
What we can say is roughly this.
Plenty of animals clearly understand that something has changed and that the change is bad. Several show behaviour indistinguishable from grief. Some use death as information about danger. A few can plan for futures they are not currently in.
What no animal has ever demonstrated is the specific, awful package that humans carry around: the knowledge that their own death is certain, that it applies to them personally, that it could be at any time, and that nothing can be done about it.
And there is a real possibility — this is the part I find genuinely unsettling — that some animals have parts of it. Not the full picture. Just enough to know that something is very wrong, without the framework to make sense of it.
An elephant standing over bones for twenty minutes might be somewhere in that gap.

## ACT 7 — WHAT IT COST US

NARRATION:
Which turns the question around, and points it at you.
Because we're the ones who definitely have it. And it is not free.
The knowledge of your own death is running in the background of everything you do. It's in the will, in the insurance, in the health check you've been putting off. It's in every religion humans have ever built — practically all of them are, among other things, an answer to this exact problem. It's in the pyramids, in the gravestones, in wanting to be remembered.
Whole cultures exist as a response to a piece of information that no other animal appears to be burdened with.
An elephant standing over the bones of her mother is, as far as we can tell, sad.
You would also be sad. And on top of that you would be doing something she almost certainly isn't: looking at those bones and understanding, precisely, that you're looking at a preview.

## RESOLUTION

NARRATION:
So — do animals know they're going to die?
The best answer available is: probably not the way you do. They have the feelings. They mostly seem to lack the sentence.
And that's the trade. They get the grief without the dread. We got the dread, and in exchange we got planning, and medicine, and history, and every attempt anybody has ever made to leave something behind.
The elephant touches the bones, and then walks away, and does not spend the afternoon thinking about herself.
There's a version of that which sounds like a loss.
And a version that sounds like mercy.
And if you're now wondering what else you have that no other animal has to carry...
...that's a question for another video.
