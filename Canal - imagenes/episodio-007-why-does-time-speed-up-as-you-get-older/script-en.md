# 007 — Why Does Time Speed Up As You Get Older?

- **Formato**: explainer ~10 min, monigotes MS Paint, segunda persona.
- **Tema**: percepción del tiempo, teoría proporcional y densidad de novedad.
- **Portada (idea)**: monigote grande de frente con cara de susto sujetando un calendario cuyas hojas salen volando. Texto: "WHERE DID IT GO?"
- **Narración**: solo se locuta lo que va bajo `NARRATION:`.

---

## COLD OPEN

NARRATION:
When you were eight, a single summer holiday lasted approximately forever.
Six weeks. You'd be a slightly different person by the end of it. The first week alone contained more than some entire years do now.
And this year? This year has been going for a while and you couldn't confidently tell me what happened in March.
Everyone notices this. Everyone assumes it's just a feeling.
It isn't just a feeling. There are two competing explanations, both of them are partly right, and one of them tells you exactly what to do about it.

## ACT 1 — IT'S NOT YOUR CLOCK

NARRATION:
First, let's kill the obvious theory, because it's wrong and it gets repeated constantly.
The idea is that your internal clock slows down as you age, so external time seems to whip past.
Researchers have tested this directly. Sit people of different ages in a room and ask them to estimate when three minutes have passed, with no clock. Older adults do this about as well as younger ones. There's some variation, but nothing remotely big enough to explain the difference between a summer at eight and a summer at forty.
And here's the detail that really rules it out: in the moment, older people don't experience time as fast. Ask someone how long this afternoon felt, and you get roughly normal answers at every age.
The distortion isn't happening live. It's happening in the rear-view mirror.
Which means whatever's going on isn't in your clock. It's in your memory.

## ACT 2 — THE PROPORTIONAL THEORY

NARRATION:
The oldest explanation is also the simplest, and it comes from a nineteenth-century philosopher called Paul Janet.
His idea: you judge a length of time against the total amount of time you've lived.
When you're one year old, a year is your entire life. Everything. When you're five, a year is a fifth of everything you've ever known — still enormous. At ten, a tenth. At forty, one fortieth.
Same three hundred and sixty-five days. Wildly different fraction of the file.
Run that logic forward and it produces an unpleasant result. If you measure life in how long it subjectively feels rather than how many years it contains, the halfway point of your life arrives at about age seven.
By twenty, you've subjectively used up most of it.
Now, this is elegant, and it's clearly capturing something real. But it can't be the whole story, and here's how you know.
The proportional theory says the speed-up should be smooth and constant, a gentle gradient across your whole life.
It isn't. People report specific stretches that flew and specific stretches that dragged. Some people have a year in their thirties that felt longer than the five around it. And almost everybody had one very recent year that behaved extremely strangely.

## ACT 3 — THE OTHER THEORY: MEMORY DENSITY

NARRATION:
So here's the second explanation, and I think it's the better one.
Your sense of how long a period of time was is reconstructed afterwards from how much you remember of it.
A stretch stuffed with distinct, retrievable memories reads as long. A stretch that produced almost nothing readable reads as short — even if it was exactly the same number of days.
And your brain is extremely selective about what it bothers to record. It records novelty. New places, new people, first times, things that broke the pattern. It does not bother recording the fourth thousand identical commute, because there's no reason to.
Now think about being a child. Everything is the first time. First time on a bike, first time at the sea, first day at a new school, first time you understood a joke an adult made. A child's year is almost entirely composed of things worth filing.
And think about a typical adult year. Same job, same route, same flat, same faces, same weekly shop.
The days were not shorter. There were just far fewer of them worth keeping.
Try it on yourself right now. Think back to a holiday you took somewhere unfamiliar — a week, maybe ten days. You can probably produce five or six distinct scenes from it. A specific meal. Getting lost. A conversation. The way a particular street looked.
Now think back to a random ordinary week from four months ago. Same seven days. Almost certainly you cannot retrieve a single specific scene from it, and if you can, it's because something unusual happened.
Your brain is not being lazy. It's being efficient. There was nothing there that it needed.
That's why the summer at eight feels enormous. It isn't nostalgia. That summer genuinely left more material behind.

## ACT 4 — THE PROOF NOBODY PLANNED

NARRATION:
And then, a few years ago, the entire planet accidentally ran the experiment.
During the lockdowns of twenty twenty, researchers surveyed thousands of people about their sense of time, and got a result that looks contradictory until you understand the two-system explanation.
Enormous numbers of people reported that the days crawled. Interminable. Nothing to do, nothing happening, an afternoon that would not end.
And the same people reported that the months had vanished. Where did the year go? What did I do? It's gone.
Slow days, fast months, at the same time, in the same person.
That's exactly what memory density predicts. In the moment, an empty day drags, because there is nothing to occupy attention and you notice every minute of it. But afterwards, that same empty day left nothing behind to measure with. Two months of identical afternoons collapse into a single vague blur.
Live time and remembered time are not the same thing, and they can move in opposite directions.
Which is the key to the whole video: the reason your years are speeding up is not that they're going faster. It's that they're leaving less behind.

## ACT 5 — WHY IT ACCELERATES SPECIFICALLY IN ADULTHOOD

NARRATION:
Put the two theories together and you can see why the effect hits hardest in a very specific window.
Childhood: high novelty, small denominator. Time is vast.
Late teens and twenties: still very high novelty. First job, first move, first relationship, first time being responsible for yourself. There's a well-documented thing in memory research called the reminiscence bump — when older adults are asked to recall their lives, a disproportionate number of vivid memories come from roughly fifteen to thirty. That period is over-represented for the rest of your life.
Then, for most people, something changes. The big firsts run out. Life becomes competent and repeated. And competence is the enemy here, because the whole point of becoming good at something is that you can do it without paying attention — and anything you did without paying attention was not written down.
That's the acceleration. Not aging. Repetition.
Which is oddly hopeful, because repetition is a thing you can change and aging isn't.

## ACT 6 — WHAT ACTUALLY WORKS

NARRATION:
So if you want your years back, the instruction that falls out of this is annoyingly specific.
It is not "slow down." It's not mindfulness, or being present, though those are fine things. Being present makes today feel longer while you're in it. It doesn't necessarily leave anything behind.
The instruction is: create memorable first times, and space them out.
A weekend somewhere you have never been will do more to lengthen your year than a fortnight in the place you go every year. The unfamiliar route home leaves a mark that the usual one doesn't. A new skill, badly done, generates enormous amounts of storable material precisely because you're bad at it and paying attention.
And the second part matters as much as the first: spread them out. Six new things across twelve months lengthens a year far more than six new things in one week, because they act as markers. Your brain measures duration partly by counting the distance between landmarks. Bunch the landmarks together and most of the year is featureless again.
The advice is boring. The mechanism is not. You are not trying to enjoy yourself more. You are trying to leave evidence.

## ACT 7 — THE UNCOMFORTABLE VERSION

NARRATION:
There's a harder implication in here and I'd rather say it than dodge it.
If the length of your life, as you'll eventually experience it looking back, is a function of how much of it you actually recorded — then a life can be long in years and short in the only currency that ends up mattering.
Twenty years of the same week is not twenty years. Not in memory. It compresses down to something you could describe in a paragraph.
And nobody chooses that. It happens by accumulation, one reasonable comfortable decision at a time, until the pattern is so smooth there's nothing left for the brain to catch on.
That's the real reason the summer at eight feels the way it does. It isn't that you were happier. It's that you were paying attention, constantly, because you had no choice — everything was new and you had no idea what was going to happen next.

## RESOLUTION

NARRATION:
So no, time isn't speeding up. Your clock is fine.
What's changed is how much of each year survives the trip into memory, and that's a function of how much of it was worth recording.
The good news is that this is one of the very few things about aging that pushes back. You cannot slow the calendar. You can absolutely change the density.
Go somewhere you've never been. Be bad at something in public. Take the wrong road home once in a while.
Your brain is keeping a record, and it only writes down the parts where it wasn't sure what was coming.
And if you're now wondering how much of last year you could actually reconstruct if somebody asked...
...that's a question for another video.
